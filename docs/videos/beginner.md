# Asylum: A Slow Start

**Duration:** about 13 minutes · **Level:** never used a tool like this before
**Goal:** hand someone with no background the mental model, walk one real task from
blank composer to merge, and finish on Devpipe so their first real project has somewhere
safe to keep its keys.

This is the patient version of the tour. It assumes nothing: not Git, not branches, not
what an agent is. Every term is defined the first time it is used, and the pace stays
deliberately slow. Conceptual scenes are drawn; every scene marked `shot:` is a recording
of the real application being driven, captured frame by frame from the running UI.

## Shot list

| ID | Visual | Chapter | Heading | Narration |
|----|--------|---------|---------|-----------|
| 01 | title | Start here | Take this slowly | Welcome to Asylum. Take this one slowly. By the end of the video you will have a clear picture of what this app is for, and you will be able to finish one real piece of work with it. You do not need to be an expert. You do not need to know Git. Every term gets explained the first time it turns up. |
| 02 | eli5 | Start here | One job, three tables | Here is the whole idea in its simplest form. Imagine you ask three people to build the same small bridge. If all three reach into one pile of blocks, their hands collide, and afterwards nobody can tell who changed what. So you give each person their own table, their own copy of the blocks, and the same instruction card. When they finish, you compare the bridges and you keep the best one. Asylum does exactly that, with coding assistants and the files in your project. |
| 03 | nouns | Start here | Project, task, run | Three words carry the entire app. A project is your box of blocks, which in practice is a folder of code on your computer. A task is the instruction card, written in ordinary language, like a note you would leave for a colleague. A run is one assistant's attempt at that task, at its own table. Give one task to three assistants and you get three runs. Project, task, run. Everything else hangs off those three words. |
| 04 | loop | Start here | Ask, race, compare, keep | The product is one loop, repeated. You open a project. You describe what should be true once the work is done. One or more assistants go away and work separately. You compare what came back. Then you keep the best one, or you send a note back and let a promising attempt try again. Every screen in this app exists either to help you see that loop, or to make it safer to run. |
| 05 | audience | Your pace | Start small, grow later | This works at any level. If you are new to programming, use one assistant for your first task and read the plain summary of what changed. If you have been writing code for twenty years, the exact branch, the folder on disk, the terminal output, and the line by line difference are all right there in front of you. It is the same workflow either way. You are only choosing how far down you want to look. |
| 06 | shot:setup | First launch | What the first screen checks | The first time you open Asylum it runs a short check before letting you start. It looks for Git, which is the tool that keeps a history of changes to code. It looks for at least one coding assistant installed on this machine that you have already signed in to. And it checks that your project's own tools actually work. Installed means the command was found. Verified means it completed a real run. Green here saves you from confusing failures later on. |
| 07 | shot:board | First launch | The Tasks board is home | This is the Tasks board, and it is where you will spend nearly all of your time. Down the left are your projects and the tasks inside them. Across the top is the composer, which is where you describe new work. Underneath, every attempt appears as its own card, showing which assistant made it, how long it took, and how it ended. Nothing important is hidden behind a summary. |
| 08 | shot:compose | Your first task | Describe the result, not the steps | Now the part most people get wrong at the start. Do not describe the steps you imagine. Describe the result you want to be true afterwards. Say what should change, name any rule that must not break, and include something you can check when it is done. Here I am asking for clearer validation messages, tests for two specific bad inputs, and no change to the public interface. Small and checkable beats large and vague every single time. |
| 09 | shot:agents | Your first task | Choosing who does the work | Below the box you pick which assistants get the job. For your very first task, choose one. Watch it work, read what it changed, and get comfortable with the shape of the loop. Later, when a problem has more than one sensible answer, pick two or three. Each one works alone and cannot see the others, so what you get back are genuinely independent attempts rather than three versions of one idea. |
| 10 | layouts | Your first task | Duel, triad, swarm | Because that choice comes up constantly, Asylum lets you save it. A duel is two assistants. A triad is three. A swarm is a larger group, with a cap on how many start at the same moment. They are just named selections, and you can make your own. There is also a limit on how many runs may go at once across the whole app, which protects both your machine and your usage allowance. |
| 11 | shot:fanout | Watch the fleet | One click, three separate attempts | When you start the task, Asylum does the setup for you. Each assistant is given its own branch and its own private copy of the project folder. Then each one is launched with your instructions. From this moment they are all working at the same time, and not one of them can disturb the others, or the folder you actually work in. |
| 12 | worktrees | What isolation means | Separate tables, one history | This is the part worth understanding properly, because it is what makes everything else safe. A branch is a named line of changes. A worktree is a second, separate copy of your project on disk, checked out from that same history. That is the separate table from the bridge story. Three assistants can edit the very same file at the very same second, because each one is touching its own copy of it. Your original folder is never in play. |
| 13 | shot:activity | Watch the fleet | Two questions that sound alike | Each card shows two things that sound similar and are not. Status is about the process. Queued, running, succeeded, failed, or cancelled. Activity is about what the assistant is doing right now. Working, blocked, done, or idle. Succeeded only means the program finished without an error. It does not mean the change is correct. Hold on to that distinction, because it is the one that catches people out. |
| 14 | shot:blocked | Watch the fleet | Blocked means it needs you | Orange, blocked, is the one to watch for. It means the assistant has stopped, asked a question, and is waiting on an answer from you. Open that run and you see the whole conversation, exactly as if you were sitting at its terminal. Answer it and it carries on. If it has gone off in the wrong direction, cancel it. Its files are kept either way, so nothing you might want is thrown away. |
| 15 | shot:review | Review the evidence | The difference is the truth | Once an attempt finishes, open Review. What you are looking at is a diff, which just means a comparison. Green lines were added. Red lines were taken away. Grey lines did not change and are only there for context. You can read it as one column, or as two side by side. Ask three questions. Did the right files change. Is anything missing. Did something unrelated get touched. A confident summary is pleasant. The difference is the evidence. |
| 16 | shot:checks | Review the evidence | Let the project check itself | You do not have to read code to judge an attempt. Press checks, and Asylum finds your project's own commands for type checking, linting, and tests, then runs them inside that attempt's private folder. Passing is real evidence, because it is your project's own rules agreeing with the change. Failing blocks the merge. If you are still learning to read code, treat this as your health signal first, and read the difference second. |
| 17 | shot:annotate | Review the evidence | Point at a line, say what is wrong | If an attempt is close but not right, you do not have to throw it out. Click the exact line and write what should be different in ordinary language. Your note is attached to that file and that line. Send your notes back and the same assistant carries on in the same folder, keeping everything it already got right. Review becomes a short conversation rather than a verdict. |
| 18 | merge | Keep the winner | Merging is your decision | When one attempt is clearly the strongest, you merge it, which means folding its changes back into your real project. Asylum shows you what is about to happen before it happens, and then waits for you to confirm. Nothing merges on its own. The attempts you did not keep stay exactly where they are until you clear them out, so changing your mind afterwards costs you nothing at all. |
| 19 | shot:vault | Devpipe | Keys your assistants use but never see | Now Devpipe, which answers a problem you meet the moment the work gets real. Your project needs secrets. Keys for services, addresses for databases, and so on. The unsafe habit is pasting them into files and prompts, where they end up in places you cannot fully recall. Devpipe is an account level vault. Asylum reads it through, and never copies it onto this machine, because two copies of one secret eventually disagree about whether it was deleted. |
| 20 | shot:grants | Devpipe | Scopes and grants, in plain terms | Entries come in two kinds. Values, which are ordinary settings, and secrets, which are protected. Each one sits at a level. Your whole account, a single workspace, or one machine. When the same name appears twice, the narrowest level wins. And a machine cannot read a secret merely because the secret exists. You grant that machine that secret, by name. So an assistant can use a key to make a real call, without ever being shown the key, and without getting to choose where it is sent. |
| 21 | safety | Safety | What is actually protecting you | Worth gathering the safety rails in one place, because they are the reason you can relax while this runs. Every attempt is isolated, so nothing collides. Nothing is merged unless you say so. A project cannot run its own setup commands until you have explicitly trusted it. Secrets are resolved outside the assistant. And every action leaves a record you can go back and read afterwards. |
| 22 | finish | Where next | Your first task, today | That is the entire loop. Open a project. Write one small task with something you can check at the end. Give it to a single assistant. Read the difference. Run the checks. Then merge it, or send a note back and let it try once more. Do that one time today, on something small and low risk, and every other part of this app will fall into place. Thanks for watching. |
